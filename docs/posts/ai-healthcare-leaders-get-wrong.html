AI in Healthcare: What Leaders Actually Get Wrong

The problem isn't the algorithm — it's the foundation. Why most healthcare AI initiatives stall at pilot, and what separates the organizations that actually scale.

Arsalan Karim · Principal Consultant, Roxen Dynamics
May 15, 2026 · 7 min read

Every healthcare organization in Canada is exploring AI. Most of them are doing it backwards. The model gets selected before the problem is defined. The pilot gets funded before the infrastructure exists to support it. The announcement gets made before anyone has asked whether the data it depends on is actually clean enough to use.

The Pilot Trap

Healthcare AI has a pilot problem. Organizations routinely stand up impressive proofs-of-concept — a readmission prediction model, an administrative automation tool, a clinical decision support engine — that perform well in a controlled environment and then go nowhere. The pilot succeeds. The scale-up fails.

This pattern is so consistent that it has its own name in the literature: the "AI chasm." The gap between a working pilot and a deployed, operationalized capability that delivers ongoing clinical or operational value.

What sits in that chasm? Almost always, the same set of foundation problems that were present before the pilot started but were invisible because the pilot was too small to surface them.

"We had a beautiful model. Our data scientists were proud of it. The problem was it was trained on data from one site, and when we tried to deploy across the network, the data structures were different at every hospital."

— Chief Digital Officer, provincial health network

The Foundation Problems

Based on our work with healthcare organizations across Canada, the barriers to AI scale cluster into four categories — and none of them are about the algorithm:

  • Data quality and governance — AI is only as good as the data it consumes. Most healthcare organizations have significant data quality issues: inconsistent coding, missing fields, siloed systems with no shared identifiers, and no governance framework for maintaining quality over time. A pilot can work around these limitations. Production cannot.
  • Integration architecture — Deploying an AI model into a clinical workflow requires integrating with EHR systems, imaging platforms, lab systems, and often external data sources. Without a mature integration architecture — APIs, event-driven messaging, standardized data contracts — AI tools become isolated point solutions rather than embedded capabilities.
  • Change management and clinical adoption — The best model in the world delivers no value if clinicians don't use it. Healthcare AI initiatives consistently underinvest in the human side of deployment: workflow redesign, clinician training, feedback mechanisms, and the ongoing monitoring needed to maintain trust and performance over time.
  • Governance and accountability structures — Who owns the model? Who monitors its performance? Who makes the call when it behaves unexpectedly? Most organizations that deploy AI have not answered these questions before go-live, which means they get answered in a crisis rather than a design session.

What Separates the Organizations That Scale

The healthcare organizations we have seen successfully scale AI capabilities — beyond individual pilots, into sustained operational value — share a set of characteristics that have less to do with their technical sophistication than their organizational preparation.

They treat AI as an architectural question, not a technology question. Before selecting a model or a vendor, they ask: does our current data infrastructure support this at scale? Does our integration layer allow this to connect to the systems it needs? Do we have the governance structures to maintain and evolve this capability over time?

They build the foundation first, or they constrain the scope of what they deploy to fit the foundation they have. They don't pretend that a pilot environment represents production conditions. And they invest in the clinical adoption work with the same discipline they bring to the technical work.

The organizations that scale AI are not more technologically advanced. They are more honest about what they are not ready for.

The Questions Worth Asking Before the Pilot

If your organization is considering an AI initiative — or trying to understand why a previous one stalled — here are the questions that should come before the model selection:

  • What is the data quality and completeness of the inputs this model will depend on — and is that quality sufficient for production, not just proof-of-concept?
  • What integration points will deployment require, and are those interfaces currently available, documented, and stable?
  • What change to clinical or operational workflow does successful adoption require, and have we designed for that change?
  • Who will own, monitor, and evolve this capability once it is live — and do those people exist and have the capacity?
  • What happens when the model performs unexpectedly — and do we have a governance process to detect that and respond?

None of these questions are about AI. They are about organizational readiness. And answering them honestly, before the pilot, is what separates the healthcare organizations that use AI as a strategic capability from the ones that spend four years running impressive pilots that go nowhere.

The Architectural Prerequisite

There is one structural recommendation that emerges consistently from this analysis: healthcare AI initiatives need an enterprise architecture perspective before they need a data science perspective. The foundational work — data governance, integration architecture, capability design — is architectural work. It requires understanding the whole system, not just the model.

Organizations that front-load this architectural thinking build AI capabilities that last. Organizations that skip it build models that impress and then disappear.
