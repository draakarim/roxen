Building a Future State Roadmap Executives Actually Approve

Roadmaps fail in the boardroom, not the delivery phase. How to frame your future state in terms that land with executives, get funded, and stay funded through the hard middle of delivery.

Arsalan Karim · Principal Consultant, Roxen Dynamics
May 2, 2026 · 4 min read

Most future state roadmaps are not rejected because they are wrong. They are rejected because they are incomprehensible to the people who need to approve them. The architects and programme leads who built them understand every assumption and dependency. The board members and executives who need to fund them do not — and should not have to.

The Translation Problem

There is a persistent gap in how transformation roadmaps are built and how they are consumed. The team building the roadmap thinks in terms of capabilities, architectures, integration sequences, and technical dependencies. The executives approving it think in terms of risk, return, competitive position, and operational continuity.

These are not incompatible ways of thinking about the same problem. They are complementary views of the same reality. The roadmap that gets approved is the one that bridges them — that shows the technical programme as an expression of strategic choices, with clear articulation of what is gained at each stage and what is risked by not proceeding.

"We kept presenting the roadmap as a technical programme. The board kept asking strategic questions we weren't answering. We eventually realized we were speaking completely different languages about the same project."

— CTO, large Canadian financial services firm

What Executives Actually Need to See

In our experience working with senior leadership teams across healthcare and financial services, executives approving a transformation roadmap need four things that most roadmaps don't deliver clearly:

  • A problem statement they recognize — Not "our current architecture cannot support the data volumes required by our digital initiative." Rather: "our operational cost per transaction is 40% above industry benchmark because of manual reconciliation processes that a modernized platform eliminates." The technical problem needs to be translated into a business problem the executive already knows they have.
  • A clear before/after — What does the organization look like at the end of the programme? Not in technical terms — in operational and strategic terms. What can we do then that we cannot do now? What risk goes away? What competitive position do we reach?
  • Staged value, not back-loaded value — Executives approve long programmes when they can see value accruing at each stage, not just at the end. A roadmap that promises everything at month 24 is a roadmap that loses funding in month 14. Show what gets better and when.
  • An honest risk view — Not a risk register with 47 items and every one rated "medium." A clear view of the two or three risks that could materially derail the programme, what is being done to mitigate them, and what the organization does if they materialize. Executives will construct their own risk view if you don't provide one — and their version will be less accurate and more conservative than yours.

The Architecture of Approval

There is a structural principle that underlies every roadmap that successfully navigates executive approval: it presents decisions, not recommendations. The roadmap team has done the analysis. The executive needs to make a choice between paths, not ratify a path that has already been chosen for them.

This is a subtle but important shift. "Here is what we are going to do" creates resistance. "Here are two viable approaches, here is what each costs and delivers, here is our recommendation and why" creates engagement. Executives who feel they have made a decision are far more likely to defend it under pressure than executives who feel they have approved someone else's plan.

The Ongoing Relationship

The approval is not the end of the challenge. Long programmes lose executive support not usually because they fail technically — but because communication breaks down. The executives who approved in month one no longer feel connected to what's happening in month eighteen. They hear about problems through the grapevine rather than through structured updates. Their confidence erodes.

The roadmaps that sustain support are the ones that come with a communication architecture: a regular cadence of executive updates that consistently frame progress in strategic terms, surface issues before they become crises, and reconnect the programme to the original business case. Not a status report — a conversation.

The boardroom presentation is not the end of the approval process. It is the beginning of an ongoing relationship between programme leadership and executive sponsorship that must be actively maintained.

The One Thing That Kills Approval

Of all the ways that roadmap presentations go wrong, one pattern is more consistently fatal than any other: the roadmap that promises everything to everyone. The one that claims to solve every organizational problem, eliminate every risk, and deliver measurable value across every business unit — all within budget and on schedule.

Experienced executives don't believe programmes that overpromise. They don't approve them with enthusiasm. They approve them with skepticism, set conservative expectations, and withdraw support the moment the first thing doesn't go to plan — because their instinct that it was oversold was right.

The roadmap that earns sustained approval is the one that is honest about what it solves and what it doesn't. That builds confidence not through ambition but through credibility. That asks executives for permission to do something specific and valuable — not everything at once.
