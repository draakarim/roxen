The Hidden Cost of Skipping Current State Assessments

Organizations that skip the diagnostic phase spend 3× more on course corrections. Here's what a real current state assessment looks like — and why it's the most undervalued step in any transformation.

Arsalan Karim · Principal Consultant, Roxen Dynamics
May 28, 2026 · 5 min read

There is a particular kind of organizational optimism that kicks in when a transformation project is approved. The budget is there. The vendor is selected. The board is aligned. The temptation to move fast — to skip the diagnostic work and get to the "real" project — is almost irresistible. And it is almost always a mistake.

The Invisible Tax

A current state assessment is not glamorous work. It does not appear in the roadmap deck presented to executives. It does not generate the kind of visible momentum that makes a project feel like it's moving. What it does — when done properly — is prevent you from building on faulty assumptions.

When organizations skip this step, they don't avoid the cost. They defer it. The diagnostic work that wasn't done in weeks one through four gets done — under pressure, at higher cost, with fewer options — in months eight, twelve, or eighteen. By then, decisions have been made, contracts have been signed, and reversing course carries an entirely different price tag.

"We thought we understood our processes. We had documented them. Then we actually walked the floor and found out the documentation was five years out of date and bore almost no resemblance to how people actually worked."

— VP of Operations, major Canadian health authority

What "Current State" Actually Means

When we talk about a current state assessment, we are not talking about a documentation exercise. We are talking about a diagnostic that answers four questions with evidence rather than assumption:

  • What do people actually do? — Not what the process map says they do. The informal workarounds, the undocumented exceptions, the department-level variations that exist in practice but not on paper.
  • What systems are actually in use? — Including the spreadsheets, the shared drives, the legacy tools that were supposed to be retired three years ago but are still running because nothing replaced them properly.
  • What does the data actually look like? — Not what it should look like per the data model. The actual quality, completeness, and consistency of data in systems that will need to be migrated or integrated.
  • What is the organization actually ready for? — Not executive appetite for change. Frontline readiness — the teams who will be most affected, the managers who will carry the change, the pockets of resistance that will surface at go-live if they're not surfaced now.

Each of these four questions sounds simple. Each of them, in practice, takes real investigation. Structured interviews. Workflow observation. System inventories. Data profiling. And a willingness to document what is actually true rather than what people wish were true.

The Compounding Problem

The reason skipping current state is so costly is that transformation projects are interdependent. A gap discovered in data quality has downstream implications for migration scope. A gap discovered in integration complexity has downstream implications for timeline. A gap discovered in frontline readiness has downstream implications for change management investment.

When these gaps are discovered early — in a structured diagnostic — they can be managed. Scope can be adjusted. Resources can be allocated. Timelines can be set realistically. The project can be designed with known constraints rather than assumed ones.

When they surface mid-project, they arrive as crises. Scope changes get managed as exceptions rather than plans. Timelines stretch under pressure. Relationships between clients and delivery teams deteriorate. The project that was going to be a success story becomes a cautionary one.

The cost of a current state assessment is fixed. The cost of not doing one is open-ended.

What a Good Assessment Produces

A rigorous current state assessment delivers three outputs that every transformation project needs before it begins:

  • A capability gap map — a structured view of where the organization's current capabilities fall short of what the future state will require, with prioritization and dependency analysis.
  • A risk register with evidence — not a generic list of transformation risks, but a specific inventory of the risks this organization faces given its actual starting conditions.
  • A realistic baseline — the foundation against which future state design can be validated, scoped, and sequenced.

These outputs don't just inform the project plan. They become the anchor that prevents scope creep, stakeholder misalignment, and the kind of mid-project pivots that drain budget and erode confidence.

The Investment Framing

One of the common objections to current state assessments is timeline — that spending two to four weeks on diagnostic work delays the "real" project. This framing misunderstands what the diagnostic phase is. It is not a delay to the project. It is the first phase of the project. The one that determines whether everything that follows will succeed or fail.

The organizations that treat it as an investment rather than an overhead consistently deliver better outcomes. Not because they are better at execution. Because they know what they are executing against.
