Why Your EHR Implementation Will Fail Before It Starts

Most failed implementations aren't execution failures — they're assessment failures. Here's what organizations get wrong before the project even kicks off.

Arsalan Karim · Principal Consultant, Roxen Dynamics
June 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Most EHR implementations don't fail because of the technology. They fail because organizations commit to a direction before understanding where they are — and that gap between assumption and reality is where projects go to die.

The Assessment Problem

When an organization decides to implement or replace an EHR system, the typical sequence goes something like this: identify a vendor, negotiate a contract, kick off the project. The current state analysis — if it happens at all — is treated as a formality, a checkbox on the project plan rather than a foundational diagnostic.

That's the trap.

A current state assessment isn't just a technical inventory. It's a diagnostic. It maps your actual workflows (not your documented ones), your real integrations (not your assumed ones), your current capability gaps, and the organizational readiness of the people who will be living with the new system. Without this baseline, every assumption you make about the future state is built on air.

"We thought we had 12 integrations. We had 47. That discovery happened in month three of an 18-month project."

— Healthcare CIO, large Canadian hospital network

What Gets Skipped

In our experience working with healthcare organizations across Canada, here's what typically gets skipped or rushed during the pre-implementation phase:

  • Workflow mapping at the unit level — not department level. The difference matters. Two units in the same department often have divergent workflows that a single EHR configuration can't accommodate without customization.
  • Shadow IT and workaround documentation — the informal systems staff have built to compensate for gaps in existing tools. These disappear when a new EHR goes live, taking institutional knowledge with them.
  • Data quality and migration complexity — legacy data is rarely clean. Understanding the scope of data remediation before migration, not during, is the difference between a controlled cutover and a crisis.
  • Change readiness at the frontline — not just leadership buy-in. Frontline clinical staff who weren't involved in the assessment become the source of resistance in go-live.
  • Technical debt in connected systems — the legacy applications that will need to interface with the new EHR often have undocumented APIs, retired integration points, or owners who have left the organization.

Each of these skips creates a risk that surfaces downstream — usually at the worst possible moment, when the project is already committed and the budget is already spent.

The Future State Trap

There's a second failure mode that often follows the first: the future state is designed in a vacuum. Organizations describe where they want to be without a grounded understanding of where they are. The result is a roadmap that looks compelling in a boardroom presentation but is functionally undeliverable given the actual starting conditions.

A real future state roadmap is built in response to the current state. It identifies the specific gaps you need to close, the sequencing that minimizes clinical disruption, and the dependencies that determine what can and can't happen in parallel. It's also honest about what won't change — not every problem needs to be solved in this project, and trying to solve all of them is often what makes projects fail.

The future state document that impresses the board is often the document that breaks the project. Ambition without a foundation is just risk with better slides.

Before You Sign the Contract

Before your organization commits to an EHR implementation or replacement, here are five questions you should be able to answer with confidence:

  • Have we mapped our current workflows at the unit level, including informal ones?
  • Do we have a complete inventory of every system that will need to interface with the new EHR?
  • Have we assessed our data quality and estimated the complexity of migration?
  • Do we understand our organizational readiness — team by team, not just at the executive level?
  • Is our future state roadmap validated against our current state findings, or is it aspirational?

If you can't answer these confidently, you're not ready to sign a contract — you're ready to commission an assessment. The two to four weeks spent doing this work properly will save months of rework and course correction downstream.

The Competitive Advantage of Preparation

The organizations that succeed with EHR implementations aren't necessarily better at execution. They're better at preparation. The investment in a rigorous current state assessment isn't a delay to the project — it's the difference between a project that delivers clinical value and one that becomes an expensive cautionary tale cited in conference presentations for the next decade.

The diagnostic phase is where the project is won or lost. Everything else is delivery.
